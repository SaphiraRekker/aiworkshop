The bigger picture

Before the demos, why this conversation matters right now, what the research says, and an honest pushback on some of the hype.

The evidence, AI use in research has exploded

Nature, April 2026, "Could agentic AI topple grant-funding systems?"

Rees & Wilsdon analysed grant applications from 12 major funders across Australia, Belgium, Canada, China, Spain, the UK and EU, including the ARC, ERC and Wellcome.

57%
increase in grant applications in 2025 vs 2022, across all 12 funders
58%
of researchers now use AI in their work, up from 37% in 2024 (Elsevier, 3,234 researchers)
5%
of EU Marie Skłodowska-Curie applications fell below quality threshold in 2025, down from 20% in 2018

The article argues this risks making grant systems unworkable, if every proposal meets the quality bar, funding becomes a lottery. The ARC is directly mentioned. This is your funding landscape right now.

→ Read the full article in Nature

The claim I disagree with ▾

💬 From the article

"They can produce tens of ideas, from which a researcher can select the best for the agent to work up into fully-formatted applications. All this can be done in minutes, and with little work by the researcher."
❌My experience: this is not what happens. Getting something genuinely good quality out of Claude takes a lot of effort, careful prompting, multiple corrections, domain expertise at every step. The Future Fellowship application you'll see in Demo 1 involved hours of iteration, ~15 corrections per section, and constant fact-checking. It was not done in minutes.
💡What AI does do is raise the floor, it's harder to submit something truly poor. But raising the ceiling still requires the researcher's intellectual contribution. That's the distinction the article blurs.
What Claude genuinely cannot do ▾

Honest limitations, worth saying upfront

❌
Write a good paper, the intellectual contribution, the argument, the research question are yours. AI can draft, but it can't think at the level your field requires.
❌
Replace the effort in grant writing, sometimes, especially at the start, it takes just as long to produce something genuinely good. The quality improves, but the human effort doesn't disappear, in fact your expertise determines the quality.
❌
Know what's actually true in your field, it will overstate, conflate, and sometimes confabulate. Without your expertise to catch it, the output is unreliable.
❌
Ask the right question in the first place, the research problem, the gap, the contribution, that's still entirely you.
✅
What it does do, handles the repetitive, the administrative, the first draft, the formatting, the communication, the use of language, checking if requirements are met. It frees your time for the things only you can do.

Setup & Privacy

Before you start, the one setting that matters, and what not to paste in. This takes 2 minutes and covers you.

Step 1, Turn off model training

Do this before your first session

1
Go to claude.ai and create a free account (or log in)
2
Click your profile icon → Settings → Privacy
3
Find "Help improve Claude" and make sure the toggle is grey (off), this stops your chats being used to train Anthropic's AI models.
The description reads: "Allow the use of your chats and coding sessions to train and improve Anthropic AI models." You want this OFF.
4
If you've had a sensitive conversation, delete it. It'll be removed from your view immediately and fully deleted from Anthropic's systems within 30 days.
Note: conversations aren't automatically deleted, the 30-day clock only starts when YOU delete them.
5
Create a Project, click "Projects" in the left sidebar → New Project
What you can and can't put in ▾

🔒 Using Claude Projects safely at UQ

✅Your own writing, drafts, ideas, grant sections, publications
✅Anonymised scenarios, "a student emailed saying they're struggling..." (no name, no ID)
✅Grant frameworks & criteria, upload the PDF directly
✅Research ideas & drafts, with training off, you're good
❌Student names, IDs, grades, never, regardless of settings
❓Participant / research data, depends on your ethics approval and consent forms. If they predate 2023 they likely don't cover AI tools. Check with UQ's Human Research Ethics unit before using.
❌Journal articles from UQ library, uploading creates an unauthorised copy and breaches copyright. Use your own pre-print or manuscript instead.
⚠️Co-investigators' unpublished ideas, check with them first
⚠️Sensitive data requiring high security, use M365 Copilot (UQ-approved for SENSITIVE) or UQ's offline AI via the Research Computing Centre instead
📋 UQ Research AI Guidance (Nov 2025): "Responsible Use of Generative AI in Research", prepared by the Director Research Environment, reviewed by PVC Research. Claude is named as a legitimate tool for research tasks. The key condition: do not share personal, sensitive or confidential information with public AI tools. For sensitive data, UQ recommends M365 Copilot (assessed for SENSITIVE classification) or UQ's offline AI capabilities via the Research Computing Centre.
Example, AI disclosure in a consultancy proposal ▾

What good disclosure looks like in practice

"This proposal was prepared in co-operation with Claude (Anthropic), an AI assistant, which was used to help structure the document, map existing research capabilities to the instructed scope, and compile preliminary computational results from the Carbon Budget Tracker pipeline (in development). The preliminary quantitative results presented in Section 3 are outputs of the computational pipeline and are included to indicate the scope of analysis that is feasible. All numbers, methodological descriptions, and analytical conclusions require detailed review and verification by the researcher before they can be relied upon in any expert report or legal proceeding."

💡 Notice what this does: names the tool, specifies exactly what it was used for, flags what still requires human verification, and sets clear limits on how the output can be relied upon. That's the standard UQ's research guidance is pointing toward.

Grant Applications

These are real prompting patterns from a Future Fellowship application, with verbatim examples. Grant writing with Claude is not prompt → output, it's an editing loop where your domain judgment is the non-negotiable ingredient.

How to set up a Claude Project

Before you type a single prompt

1
Write the project description, Claude reads this before every prompt. Keep it brief to start and build on it as you go. Here's a real example:
"This project is for drafting my ARC Future Fellowship application (UQ-FT26). My (Dr Saphira Rekker) research is on benchmarking corporate emissions against different climate scenarios, focusing on cumulative emissions and carbon budgets. I make this research accessible through the Carbon Budget Tracker, the CBT is a currently a working prototype that has assessed 10 steel and power companies as a demo, against 4 scenarios, using 2 allocation methods. Working preferences: - spell out acronyms on first use - prefer understatement to overstatement - no em-dashes - if you're uncertain about a number or claim, ask before writing it - ask clarifying questions before drafting anything substantive"
2
Upload the documents an inexperienced research assistant would need to do the job, grant guidelines, your prior applications, publications, track record, support letters. If an RA would need it, Claude needs it.
Think of Claude as a very capable but inexperienced RA at the start. The more you load in and correct, the more it evolves, with a well-set-up Project, it starts behaving more like a postdoc who knows your work.
3
Start prompting, and keep updating as you go, a Project is not set and forget. Add new documents as they arrive, update the instructions when you learn something, add rules when Claude gets something wrong twice.
e.g. First week: grant docs only → after reviewer feedback: add tracked comment files → after learning Claude overstates: add the accuracy rule
Prompt 1, Upload the rules docs, ask Claude to synthesise
"I'm starting an ARC Future Fellowship application (UQ-FT26). I've attached four guidance documents: the Detailed Assessor Handbook, Instructions to Applicants, NIT Writing Guide, and UQ How-to Guide. Read all four and produce a concise rules document I can save and reuse for every chat in this project. Cover: assessment criteria with weights, section requirements with character limits, NIT guidance, and any UQ-specific rules. Ask me anything you need clarified before producing it."
💡 Tell Claude the deliverable's shape, how to use the documents, and ask it to ask questions before drafting. The output becomes the foundation document for the entire application.
Prompt 2, Map the background docs, establish usage rules
"I'm working on my ARC Future Fellowship application (UQ-FT26). The FF is a follow-up from my DECRA (DE240100533), treat the DECRA as foundational: drawable for content and voice, but never citable. Rules summary is already in the project. Map the background docs I've attached (not the templates, Excel, or official rules PDFs). For each, tell me what you infer (what it is, authorship, likely use); ask me to confirm or correct; then produce a single reference doc with roles and usage rules. Create an .md doc that I can add to the project files and how I should make a note of this in the project description. No drafting of the application yet."
💡 This is upload + usage rules in action, Claude maps what it's been given, checks its understanding before assuming, and produces a reusable reference document. "No drafting yet" is key: you're building the foundation before any writing starts.

Every chat, keep the Project alive

A Project is not set and forget. After each conversation, ask yourself:

  • Update the project description, add new rules you've learned, correct anything that's drifted, capture decisions made
  • Add new files, documents that arrived (reviewer feedback, support letters, tracked comments), or documents Claude created that you want to reuse (rules summaries, reference docs, drafted sections)
Each session, how to start ▾
Prompt 3a, Starting from scratch: front-load the narrative, ask Claude to ask questions
"Apply FT26_rules.md and FT26_documents.md. Working on C1 project description today. I think we start with the diagram in the NCC comment (eProofingNCCComment), we generated momentum with the whole annex I wrote for ISO to at least have a carbon budget approach. Then we need to talk about what we have done as part of the DECRA and what's happened over the past 2 years. Also connecting with people working on different pieces of the puzzle. Research conducted since the DECRA: 1) Assessing compatibility of transition plans with equity [doc attached], led by me, includes someone who leads the ISO equity group and equity/pathway scholars at the IPCC modelling group IMAGE in the Netherlands 2) Company data availability piece [PCP project description + David Kampmann doc attached], a lot of manual work, how do we have accurate company data? 3) Complexity of underlying pathways, so many with different data availability (sectors, regions). Also national transition plans, working with Adrien Rose, PhD student at LSE [working paper attached], who now co-leads the CarbonBudgetTracker (new name for AreYouParisCompliant) 4) Allocation methods, covered in the equity piece Litigation people have been very keen on our continued research [see ROPE doc]. Please ask me questions on where the platform is at, anything you need to help write the project description. Don't draft yet."
💡 Give Claude the narrative arc + attach all evidence docs + invert the interaction ("ask me questions"). This turns a generation task into a planning conversation. A cold "write my C1" produces plausible-sounding text without the texture of what you've actually built.
Prompt 3b, When you have a draft: assess before redrafting
"Apply FT26_rules.md and FT26_documents.md. Working on [section] today. Attached: the template and my current draft. Read the template, find the relevant requirements in the rules, read my current draft, then tell me where the draft is over- or under-investing relative to what the section needs to do. Don't redraft yet, assess first."
💡 The "don't redraft yet" is doing a lot of work, it forces diagnosis before prescription. Once you have the assessment, iterate from there.

⚠️ Honest caveat

💬Claude sometimes confabulates specifics from earlier in a conversation, inventing a plausible-sounding detail that is wrong. Verify anything Claude cites as coming from "our earlier conversation" before using it in a submission. Your expertise is the check on Claude's confidence.

Project Management, where it gets interesting

It's debatable how much time the Future Fellowship application saved me. But here's what happened next.

🎯 The actual prompt that started it

"I am presenting tomorrow to the 'team', it will include a new research assistant who knows NOTHING about this field. We need two slides/diagrams, 1 very very simple to a layman what we are doing and why, then get into the details of who is doing what. We can also have a little abstract or diagram of all the papers we are working on."

One line. No specification of format, colours, interactivity, or structure. The reason it worked: the Project already contained the DECRA materials, the Future Fellowship project description, the ROPE, and the NCC comment. A short prompt only works because the context was already loaded. Without that, Claude would have asked basic questions instead of drafting.

💬 What happened next

"The first version was React, which Claude does by default. Sharing it with the team turned out to be harder than the building. We tried StackBlitz URLs that didn't work, hit dead ends, and eventually built a standalone HTML version as a backup. The HTML version was what actually got used. The lesson isn't 'Claude picked the right format' — the lesson is 'have a sharing fallback because the fancy option often breaks.'"

Which Project did I use? ▾

💡 An honest reflection

The CBT dashboard was built inside the Future Fellowship Project, not a separate CBT Project. That was the right pragmatic call in the moment: the dashboard needed to be ready the next day, and all the context was already there. The one-line prompt worked because the FF project already knew the research.

But in hindsight, the CBT deserves its own Project, it's ongoing, it has different collaborators, and it keeps growing (Discovery Project, outreach emails, conference follow-ups). The lesson: start where the context is, then create a dedicated home as the work matures.

That migration is easy, just create a new Project and copy across the relevant documents.

What to load into a dedicated project management Project ▾

For team briefings, dashboards and ongoing project work

1
Your project description or proposal, even a rough version. This is what let Claude explain the research to a layman without asking what the research was.
2
A current status note, what's live, what's in progress, what's aspirational. Claude cannot distinguish these without you telling it.
3
Your team and their roles, who's doing what. Claude will invent plausible team structures if this isn't explicit.
4
Your papers list with status, published, under review, in progress. Claude can then generate a publications overview without you listing them manually each time.
How it was built, small, specific corrections ▾
What the first draft looked like
Three slides: big picture, who-does-what architecture, papers overview. Structurally correct but with domain errors only the researcher could catch, wrong labels, conflated concepts, inaccurate status tags.
💡 The first draft is never finished. It's a starting point that your expertise then corrects.
The actual correction prompts, verbatim, typos and all
1. "NGFS 2C, >2C is not a priority, national context comes first!" 2. "I would divide context-specific into NDCs/plans, and equity." 3. "should we actually maybe move from bottom to top... like start with platform and then go into the ingrediteds we need..." 4. "Felesina is his last name, [her] name is wrong too" (correcting team member names) 5. "we have more papers :) https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-31143-4 for example and https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-023-01734-0"
💡 Lowercase, typos, casual register. Each one fixed something specific. This is what most iteration looks like, not carefully crafted prompts, just you talking to a colleague. Never "redo the whole thing", point at one thing at a time.
Where Claude failed
Failure 1: When collapsible sections were added, Slide 1 was dropped entirely. The display broke and had to be patched back in. Failure 2: Sharing. The .jsx file couldn't be opened by the team. Two wrong StackBlitz URL formats before the right one worked. Eventually rebuilt as a standalone HTML file, which is what actually got used.
💡 Lesson 1: big structural rewrites of a working file are risky, small incremental edits are safer. Lesson 2: always have a sharing fallback because the fancy option often breaks.

Anchor 2, The Discovery Project ▾
The opening prompt
"Hi! We are now applying also for a discovery project, which will focus on the scenario problem particularly, we can have up to 2 pages for the project description, this is what we have now. Can you help complete this? Please review the content in relation to scenario analysis from the future fellowship, and ask me questions before drafting."
💡 Two sentences plus an instruction to ask questions first. The project already contained the FF, NCC comment, DECRA, and equity work, so Claude could position the Discovery as distinct from but complementary to the FF without you having to explain any of it.

Format → Funder → Funder

The FF asked "are companies Paris-aligned?" The Discovery asks "how do organisations build credible climate scenarios?" Same evidence base, pathway expertise, CBT methodology, structured pluralism argument, equity work. Different scheme, different collaborator, different research question. The baseline was much higher than a cold start because the project already knew the substance.

Anchor 3, PRI conference follow-up emails ▾
The opening prompt
"Ok, so we went to PRI conference and have a demo of the CarbonBudget Tracker, now we want to follow up with people to set up a call for the demo, I will feed examples of emails my PhD student has sent out [followed by their email template]" Then for each contact: "now an email to [contact] from Clarity AI, he was quite enthusiastic and they seem a small new initiative with money" "we need to email [contacts] from TPI (a sort of competitor) but Im friendly with them and know them well and I think we should show them what we have" "can you also do one for [contact], he ran the panel on '2050 targets fit for purpose', I had a chat with him at PRI"
💡 Each prompt is one or two sentences plus a relationship cue ("friends," "small new initiative," "ran the panel on X"). The project knew the substance, CBT positioning, the science, the FF framing, publications list. A PhD student's template gave the structural register. Each new prompt added only the thing the project couldn't infer: who is this person and what's our relationship?

Same science, five different doors in

For peer competitors who are also friends, collegial and exploratory: "Since we're all working in the corporate climate assessment space, I wanted to follow up and share what we've been building, partly because I think you'd find it interesting, and partly because I'd genuinely value your perspectives."

For an investor who chaired a panel on 2050 targets, anchored on his panel: "It was great to chat with you after your panel on whether 2050 targets are fit for purpose. Rather than focusing solely on end-point targets like 'net zero by 2050,' we assess corporate climate alignment based on cumulative emissions against carbon budgets."

Same scientific content. Same platform. Different doors in. When the project knows the substance, the prompt only needs to carry the audience-specific information.

💡 Try it yourself

💬Load your project documents into a Claude Project first. Then try: "I need to brief [my team / my supervisor / my industry partner] on where this project is at. Create a simple visual overview, what we're doing and why, who is doing what, and what's published or in progress." Then correct it one thing at a time.

Frequent Responses & Signatures

Set up reusable response templates and signatures once in your Project instructions, then invoke them with a single line in any conversation.

The prompt
Prompt, ask for the templates
"I get high volumes of PhD enquiries that fall into roughly 3-4 categories: clear no-alignment, possible alignment that needs more info, and strong fits with funding. Help me draft a template for each, in my voice, that I can paste from my email signature. Also can you help me with important questions to assess possible alignment and add this at the end of the signature? Keep the tone direct and warm, no dashes."
💡 Claude already knows your research and voice from the Project, so this one prompt produces four ready-to-use templates and a question set.

The output, four templates + question set

This is what came back. Show this to the audience as the payoff.

Happy to help. Here are four templates covering the categories you described, with a question set at the end you can attach to the "possible alignment" template.
Template A, No alignment (polite decline)
Thank you for your enquiry. Unfortunately, your research interests don't align with my supervision area. I work specifically on corporate climate accountability, benchmarking company emissions against Paris-aligned carbon budgets through the Carbon Budget Tracker (CBT). I wish you well with your PhD application. Best regards, Saphira
Template B, Possible alignment (needs more info)
Thank you for your interest in PhD supervision. To help me assess fit, please reply with answers to the questions below. A few things to note before you do: • I supervise students contributing to my existing research program on the Carbon Budget Tracker (CBT), not independent thesis topics. • International students typically require a UQ Graduate School Scholarship, which is highly competitive and requires a strong publication record. • The work requires quantitative skills and programming in R or Python. See the questions at the end of this email. Best regards, Saphira
Template C, Strong fit with funding (schedule call)
Thank you for your enquiry. Your background and funding situation look relevant, and I'd like to have a brief conversation to explore this further. Please suggest a few times for a video call. I'm based in Brisbane (AEST). Before we meet, it would help if you could prepare a short presentation (10 to 15 minutes) structured around the questions at the end of this email. Best regards, Saphira
Template D, Mass email or wrong name (brief decline)
Thank you for your enquiry. My research focuses on corporate climate accountability through the Carbon Budget Tracker (CBT), which doesn't appear to align with your interests. I wish you well with your PhD application. Best regards, Saphira
Questions to assess possible alignment (attach to Templates B and C)
  1. Your current institution, position, and highest degree completed.
  2. Your peer-reviewed publications, including first-author, co-author, or under review. If none, please say so.
  3. Your programming experience in R, Python, or other relevant languages.
  4. Which CBT-related publications you have read, and what specifically interests you about this research program.
  5. What sector, region, or methodological angle you would want to contribute to, and how your background equips you to do this work.
  6. Your intended funding pathway (UQGSS, CSC, other government scholarship, industry sponsorship, or self-funded), and expected start date.
"Let me know if you'd like to adjust the tone or add categories (e.g. visiting scholars, postdoc enquiries)."

What I'd Do Differently

Three honest lessons from a year of using Claude for research.

1
Be more patient, don't rush to drafting. Spend more time on the setup: project description, usage rules, document mapping. The investment upfront pays back in every subsequent chat.
2
Ask Claude more questions about how to best set it up, Claude can tell you what it needs to do a better job. "What information would help you do this more accurately?" is a prompt worth using before you start any new project.
3
Don't go too crazy, it is easy to get carried away. You will still need to check every output before it goes out. The time you save prompting, you spend reviewing.
